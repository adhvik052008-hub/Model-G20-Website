About the conference

A smaller room, a harder brief

School of Raya Model G20 2026 simulates the world's most consequential economic forum. Twenty delegations, eight committees, one weekend, and the standing requirement that whatever you write, everyone signs.

01

Why a G20?

The G20 is where the decisions that move markets, climate policy and development finance are actually taken. It has no charter, no veto and no secretariat of its own — only the obligation to agree.

It is where the weight sits

G20 members account for around 85% of global GDP and two-thirds of the world's population. What the room agrees, the world implements.

Consensus, not majority

There is no vote to hide behind. A communiqué carries only if every delegation accepts it, which changes how you argue from the first word.

Evidence beats rhetoric

Delegates arrive with figures, not adjectives. The best speech in a G20 room is the one that survives the question that follows it.

Real agendas, current year

Every agenda on our committee list is a live question in international policy right now — not a historical set piece with a known answer.

02

Why not a
traditional MUN?

Nothing is wrong with Model UN. It is simply a different exercise, and after a few conferences most delegates have learned what it teaches.

  1. 01

    The room is too large to be accountable

    A hundred and ninety-three seats means most delegates speak twice and vote once. In a twenty-seat room there is nowhere to sit quietly, and no resolution passes without your line in it.

  2. 02

    Procedure crowds out substance

    Points, motions and unmoderated caucuses can consume a session before anyone has made an argument. We keep procedure light on purpose so the debate is about the agenda.

  3. 03

    Blocs form by habit, not by interest

    Large committees reward joining the biggest group early. A G20 table has no safe bloc — the same country is your ally on climate finance and your opponent on trade.

  4. 04

    A passed resolution is not an outcome

    Majority voting lets a committee adopt a document half the room never accepted. Consensus forces the harder, more honest work of actually changing someone's mind.

03

Why choose a G20 over a traditional MUN?

Side by side, the difference is not prestige. It is how much of the weekend you spend actually negotiating.

  Traditional MUN Model G20
Room size 40 – 190 delegates One seat per delegation
Speaking time Two or three interventions You are on the record all weekend
Decision rule Simple or two-thirds majority Consensus — everyone signs or nobody does
Procedure Heavy: points, motions, caucuses Light, so the time goes on substance
Agendas Often historical or well-rehearsed Live questions in current policy
Preparation Position paper, bloc research Position paper plus the numbers to defend it
What you leave with Experience of a large committee A document you personally negotiated

If you have never sat in a committee before, this is still the right room — three of our eight committees are graded Beginner precisely so that a first conference can be a G20.
